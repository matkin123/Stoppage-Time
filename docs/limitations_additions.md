The scoring model is Poisson, which treats goals as independent draws at a fixed per-minute rate. Football goals are not independent: a goal changes tactics, momentum, and scoreline pressure, so the rate for the next goal is conditional on what just happened. Two things make Poisson a defensible choice here anyway. The omitted windows are short, so the expected extra goals per match is small (around 0.3), and at that scale the probability of at least one goal is governed mostly by that expected count, a rate-times-exposure quantity that is the same under any goal distribution with the same mean. The independence assumption enters only the second-order correction. A fuller treatment, hierarchical and Bayesian with correlated, state-dependent rates, would sharpen that correction, but because the headline is an average over 314 matches it should shift the aggregate modestly rather than to a different regime. It would matter far more for predicting any single match, which this model does not attempt.

The result figure asks one thing more of the model than the scoreline does, so I report it separately. Flipping a scoreline needs one more goal by either side. Flipping a result also needs to know which side scores it. That extra demand touches only part of the figure: of the 13.0% flip, 7.9 points come from matches level at 90, where any goal flips regardless of scorer, and 5.1 points from one-goal games, where team quality decides who equalizes. I tested that 5.1-point slice directly (ADR-0034): swapping the flat 50/50 split for a per-match, Elo-conditioned one moves the result figure by at most 0.4 points. So the attribution channel is bounded empirically and the goal-generation assumption structurally, and neither points to a materially different aggregate.

The six tournaments are pooled but not identical — owed stoppage runs far higher in Copa América 2024 (25.3 min/match) and AFCON 2023 (23.5) than in the Euros (about 15) or the 2018 World Cup (12.7). But the model pools only one thing across them: the added-time goals-per-live-minute rate, applied to each match's own omitted minutes. It never conditions on team quality, so the question isn't whether the teams are evenly matched but whether that rate holds — and re-sourcing it from PRE-only, POST-only, or regime-matched cohorts keeps the headline inside its band. I pool the six because I need the sample; forced to widen, I'd add regions (Copa, AFCON, the Euros) over eras (pre-2018), because football varies less across space than time — the same players fill these squads from the same club leagues, so region now marks a federation more than a style.

Finally, the model prices an omitted minute as an average minute of the same half. It does not condition on the match-specific factors that shape real added time: substitutions and positional changes, player fitness and injuries, bench strength, weather, pitch condition. Those belong to a match-by-match prediction. The headline is an average over 314 matches, where such factors enter the observed scoring rates in aggregate but are never modeled one match at a time.
